Explaining the Unexplainable

Teaching smart fraud models to show their thinking, so a machine’s split-second decision can still be defended to a customer, an auditor, and a regulator.

By Minenhle Mpulo Approx. 4 min read

Every hour, millions of card transactions move through payment networks, and each one is checked for fraud. Picture a client logging into their banking app in Johannesburg, then tapping their card in Cape Town five minutes later. No one travels that fast, so the system flags it. At two in the morning you want that blocked automatically. The hard part comes later, when the client calls or the auditors arrive and ask a simple question: why did you block it?

For years, banks have answered this question with fraud systems built on clear, rule-based logic. We trust them because we can trace exactly why each decision was made and explain it to a customer or a regulator. The trouble is that they mostly recognise fraud we have seen before, while criminals keep inventing new tricks.

We still catch the criminal. But the distortion destroys the forensic evidence we need to prove it.

A newer generation of systems, often called artificial intelligence (AI), is far better at catching those new tricks because it learns the subtle patterns in how people actually spend and keeps adapting as fraud changes. The catch is that this cleverness comes in a kind of black box. You get sharper detection, but the system cannot easily tell you why it made a call. In a courtroom that evidence would be inadmissible, and a bank faces the same test every time a customer disputes a block or a regulator asks for the reason. My research is about opening that box: keeping the sharper detection while making the reasoning clear enough to defend.

01

Why a right answer is not enough

In a regulated bank, a fraud decision has to be both correct and defensible. The newest models are brilliant at the first part and quietly weak at the second.

The trusted way

Rule-based systems

Logic you can read line by line. When a transaction is blocked, an analyst can point to the exact rule and explain it to the customer. The weakness: these systems mainly catch fraud they have already been taught, so a genuinely new tactic can slip past.

The powerful way

AI systems

These learn the shape of normal behaviour and spot what does not fit, adapting as spending patterns change. They catch far more of the new tricks. The weakness: their reasoning is hidden inside a black box, so a correct block can be impossible to justify after the fact.

My work sits in the gap between these two columns: keeping the detective power of the second while recovering the accountability of the first.

What everyone sees
Visible success

The model scores well. Fraud is caught. Every dashboard says the system is performing, and on the numbers that get reported, it is.

What stays hidden
Hidden collapse

Underneath, the model’s internal picture of fraud has flattened so far that every case looks the same, and the explanations quietly stop meaning anything.

02

The ideas, in plain language

No mathematics required. Tap any idea to open it.

A modern fraud model builds an internal map of behaviour. Every transaction becomes a point on that map, and normal spending clusters together while unusual activity drifts to the edges. The model does not store rules; it stores a sense of shape. When a new transaction lands far from where it should, the model treats it as suspicious.

The map is the model’s “thinking.” The whole challenge of my research is that this map can become so compressed that it stops being readable, even though the model still detects fraud well.

When a rule-based system blocks a card, you can trace the decision. When an AI system blocks a card, the reasoning is spread across thousands of tiny internal signals that do not translate into a sentence a human can use. That is the “black box.”

In most industries this is an inconvenience. In banking it is a compliance risk, because the law and the regulator expect a clear reason for any automated decision that affects a customer.

To make a model focus on rare fraud, the standard trick is to turn up the weight on fraud examples during training. It works for detection. But I found that turning it up too hard makes the model crush its internal map of fraud down to almost a single line.

When that happens, every fraud case looks nearly identical from the inside, so the tools we use to explain decisions produce the same flat answer for every alert. The model looks confident and stable, but its explanations have quietly become useless. I call this combination the performance masking effect: the headline accuracy hides the damage underneath.

Instead of crushing the fraud map, I anchor it to the shape of normal behaviour. The model is still pushed to separate fraud from legitimate spending, but it is no longer allowed to collapse fraud into a single point.

The result is a map that keeps its structure. Detection stays just as accurate, but the explanations come back to life: different kinds of fraud once again produce different, specific reasons. Accuracy and accountability stop being a trade-off.

An automated system blocks the suspicious Johannesburg-to-Cape-Town transaction instantly, which is exactly what you want. The next morning, the customer phones and the auditor asks for the reason. This is the moment the decision goes on trial.

With a collapsed model, the honest answer is “the fraud score was 0.99, but we cannot say why.” That is the equivalent of inadmissible evidence: a correct outcome the bank cannot actually defend. With the approach in this research, the system can point to the specific pattern that triggered the block. That is the difference between a defensible decision and a liability.

03

How the study was done

Five fraud-detection models, two real-world imbalance levels, and three things measured at once: did it detect fraud, did its internal map stay healthy, and could its decisions be explained.

Compare five models on equal footing

Five variants were trained under identical conditions, from a plain baseline to the standard high-weighting approach, two alternative designs, and the proposed anchored model.

Use two genuinely hard datasets

A real European card-fraud dataset where fraud is 1 in 578 transactions, and a second dataset at 1 in 172. These are the brutal imbalance levels real fraud teams face.

Measure the internal map, not just the score

Alongside detection accuracy, the study measured how many dimensions the fraud map actually used, a direct read on whether the representation had collapsed.

Check whether explanations stayed meaningful

Finally, the study tested whether the explanations differed from one fraud case to the next, or whether they had flattened into the same answer every time.

66%
collapse in the fraud map’s usable structure under the standard high-weighting approach
3.9×
recovery of that structure with the anchored model, at the hardest imbalance level
0Δ
meaningful loss in detection accuracy when switching to the anchored approach

In short: the same detection power, a healthy internal map, and explanations that became specific again rather than identical.

04

Under the hood

For the technically curious. This is the computer-science framing behind the plain-language story above.

The model: a Variational Autoencoder

A VAE learns to compress each transaction into a small set of latent coordinates and reconstruct it. The cloud of fraud points in that latent space is the “map.” Its health is measured by effective rank, the number of dimensions the cloud genuinely occupies.

The failure: gradient variance amplification

Ratio-weighted training scales the loss on rare fraud by the imbalance ratio r. Rare, large, ratio-scaled gradients accumulate in Adam’s second-moment estimate and suppress the learning of latent variance.

g minority = (r / 2)(σ² − 1)  ⇒  E[g²] ∝ r²

The symptom: selective collapse

The minority (fraud) manifold contracts toward effective rank 1 while the majority stays well-conditioned. Detection (AUPRC) survives because separability survives, but SHAP attributions become near-identical across fraud cases. Stability scores rise for the wrong reason.

The fix: a Mahalanobis-anchored prior

Replace the ratio-weighted KL with a divergence toward a structured majority Gaussian N(μ maj, Σ maj). Its covariance term rewards using every active dimension, preserving rank without amplifying gradients.

KL = ½[ log(|Σ maj|/|Σ q|) − D + tr(Σ maj⁻¹Σ q) + (μ q−μ maj)ᵀΣ maj⁻¹(μ q−μ maj) ]

Full methodology, the five-model comparison, ablations, and significance testing appear in the paper · Springer LNAI, ICCCI 2026.

05

What comes next

This work now becomes the foundation of my PhD, and the next stage is the part I find most exciting.

Today, when criminals invent a new fraud pattern, we more or less have to take the whole system back to the workshop and retrain it from scratch, which is slow and costly. My aim is to build fraud systems that adapt on the fly.

The moment an analyst confirms a new kind of fraud, the system would adjust only the small part of its understanding that needs to change, while continuing to run and protect customers without interruption. It could even describe what it is seeing in plain terms, for example that a new pattern looks like it is shifting away from cloned-card fraud toward a digital-wallet scam, giving fraud teams a head start instead of waiting for a full investigation.

The goal is fraud defences that learn and grow at the same speed as the criminals they are built to stop.
